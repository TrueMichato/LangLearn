# Brazilian vs. European Portuguese (Português Brasileiro vs. Europeu)

<!-- vocab-inventory: {"mode":"explicit"} -->

Brazilian Portuguese (PT-BR) and European Portuguese (PT-PT) are the same language, but with significant differences in pronunciation, vocabulary, grammar, and daily usage. Think of it like American English vs. British English — mutually intelligible, but noticeably different. This lesson highlights the key differences you'll encounter.

## Pronunciation Differences

### Vowels
<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-vowel-reduction","kind":"concept","format":"qa","priority":"optional","sourceSection":"Vowels","rule":"Unstressed-vowel reduction differs by variety and region","example":"How do unstressed vowels commonly differ between European and Brazilian Portuguese?","answer":"European Portuguese generally reduces them more strongly; Brazilian realizations also vary and can reduce vowels.","explanation":"The contrast is a broad pronunciation tendency, not a rule that every Brazilian vowel is open."} -->

European Portuguese generally reduces unstressed vowels more strongly, making words sound more compressed. Brazilian Portuguese also reduces vowels, with considerable regional variation; these are broad contrasts, not a rule that all Brazilian vowels are open:

- **telefone**: BR *teh-leh-FOH-nee* vs. PT *tluh-FOHN*
- **português**: BR *por-too-GEHZ* vs. PT *pur-too-GEHSH*
- **menino**: BR *meh-NEE-noo* vs. PT *mnEE-noo*

### The Letter "t" and "d" Before "i" and "e"
<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-palatalization","kind":"concept","format":"qa","priority":"optional","sourceSection":"The Letter \"t\" and \"d\" Before \"i\" and \"e\"","rule":"Brazilian t/d palatalization before an i sound is regional, not universal","example":"Must every Brazilian pronounce tia with a ch sound?","answer":"No. Many Brazilian varieties palatalize t and d before an i sound, including some unstressed e, but regional pronunciations differ.","explanation":"The lesson's ch/j examples illustrate a common Brazilian pattern, not a universal national pronunciation."} -->

In many Brazilian varieties, "t" becomes "ch" and "d" becomes "j" before an **i sound**, including unstressed "e" when pronounced like i. Other Brazilian regions keep different realizations:

- **tia** (aunt): BR *CHEE-ah* vs. PT *TEE-ah*
- **dia** (day): BR *JEE-ah* vs. PT *DEE-ah*
- **noite** (night): BR *NOY-chee* vs. PT *NOYT*
- **cidade** (city): BR *see-DAH-jee* vs. PT *see-DAHD*

### The Letter "s" at End of Syllables
<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-final-s","kind":"concept","format":"qa","priority":"optional","sourceSection":"The Letter \"s\" at End of Syllables","rule":"Syllable-final s has regional realizations in Brazil","example":"Can a syllable-final sh-like s occur in Brazil as well as Portugal?","answer":"Yes. It is common in Rio de Janeiro and some other Brazilian varieties; other varieties use an s-like sound.","explanation":"Regional variation crosses the simple Brazil/Portugal contrast."} -->

In Rio de Janeiro and parts of Brazil, final "s" is pronounced "sh" (like in European Portuguese). In São Paulo and most of Brazil, it's a plain "s":

- **festas**: Rio/PT *FESH-tahsh* vs. SP *FES-tahs*
- **mesmo**: Rio/PT *MEHZH-moo* vs. SP *MEHZ-moo*

### Example Sentences

- **BR: Vou pegar o ônibus para o trabalho.** (voh peh-GAR oo OH-nee-boos) — I'll take the bus to work.
- **PT: Vou apanhar o autocarro para o trabalho.** — I'll take the bus to work.

- **BR: Esqueci meu celular no banheiro.** (es-keh-SEE meu seh-loo-LAR) — I forgot my cell phone in the bathroom.
- **PT: Esqueci o meu telemóvel na casa de banho.** — I forgot my phone in the bathroom.

- **BR: Que legal! Vamos tomar um sorvete?** (kee leh-GOW) — How cool! Shall we have some ice cream?
- **PT: Que fixe! Vamos comer um gelado?** — How cool! Shall we have some ice cream?

## Grammar Differences

### "Você" vs. "Tu"

<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-tu-agreement","kind":"rule","format":"qa","priority":"essential","sourceSection":"\"Você\" vs. \"Tu\"","rule":"Tu agreement varies in regional speech; standard writing uses queres","example":"How should a learner interpret informal Tu quer ir alongside standard Tu queres ir?","answer":"Tu quer ir is common regional informal usage; standard written tu agreement is queres, while você uses quer.","explanation":"Tu is used in parts of Brazil as well as Portugal; agreement and social context vary."} -->

In Brazil, **você** is the standard "you" in most regions. In Portugal, **tu** is the standard informal "you":

- **BR: Você quer ir ao cinema?** (vo-SEH kehr eer) — Do you want to go to the movies?
- **PT: Tu queres ir ao cinema?** — Do you want to go to the movies?

Note: Some regions of Brazil use **tu**, with agreement patterns that vary locally. **Tu quer ir?** is common regional informal speech; **tu queres ir?** is standard written tu agreement. Choose for the local relationship and register rather than judging speakers.

### "A gente" vs. "Nós"

<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-a-gente","kind":"rule","format":"cloze","priority":"essential","sourceSection":"\"A gente\" vs. \"Nós\"","rule":"'A gente' as informal 'we'","example":"___ ___ vai ao cinema hoje. (We, informal BR)","answer":"A gente","explanation":"A gente often means we in informal Brazilian usage and takes third-person singular agreement; it is not absent from Portugal."} -->
<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"rule-a-gente-agreement","kind":"concept","format":"qa","priority":"essential","sourceSection":"\"A gente\" vs. \"Nós\"","rule":"A gente takes third-person singular agreement","example":"In informal Brazilian Portuguese, which verb person and number does the common colloquial replacement for nós take?","answer":"Third-person singular (a gente vai), not the nós form.","explanation":"A gente means we but conjugates like ele/ela: A gente vai ao parque amanhã."} -->

In informal Brazil, **a gente** (lit. "the people") is a frequent replacement for **nós** (we). It takes third person singular conjugation. It also occurs in Portugal; the contrast is a preference, not exclusivity:

- **BR: A gente vai ao parque amanhã.** (ah JEN-chee vai) — We're going to the park tomorrow.
- **PT: Nós vamos ao parque amanhã.** — We're going to the park tomorrow.
- **BR: A gente se diverte muito lá.** (ah JEN-chee see jee-VEHR-chee) — We have a lot of fun there.

### Gerund vs. "a + infinitive"

<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-progressive","kind":"rule","format":"cloze","priority":"essential","sourceSection":"Gerund vs. \"a + infinitive\"","rule":"Brazilian gerund vs. European 'a + infinitive'","example":"(BR) Eu ___ ___ português. (I am studying)","answer":"estou estudando","explanation":"Brazilian Portuguese commonly prefers estar + gerund; European Portuguese commonly prefers estar a + infinitive, with regional exceptions."} -->
<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"rule-ep-progressive","kind":"concept","format":"qa","priority":"essential","sourceSection":"Gerund vs. \"a + infinitive\"","rule":"European Portuguese commonly uses estar a + infinitive for the progressive","example":"How does European Portuguese commonly form the progressive?","answer":"Estar a + infinitive (estou a falar).","explanation":"PT Estou a falar contigo corresponds to BR Estou falando com você; this is a preference, not a ban on European gerunds."} -->

This is one of the most immediately recognizable differences:

These are common preferences, not a ban on European gerunds; regional and constructional variation exists.

- **BR: Estou falando com você.** (es-TOH fah-LAN-doo) — I'm talking to you. *(gerund)*
- **PT: Estou a falar contigo.** — I'm talking to you. *(a + infinitive)*

- **BR: Ele está comendo.** (EH-lee es-TAH koh-MEN-doo) — He's eating.
- **PT: Ele está a comer.** — He's eating.

- **BR: O que você está fazendo?** — What are you doing?
- **PT: O que é que estás a fazer?** — What are you doing?

### Object Pronoun Placement

<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-clitic","kind":"rule","format":"cloze","priority":"essential","sourceSection":"Object Pronoun Placement","rule":"Brazilian speech commonly uses proclisis where European affirmative clauses use enclisis","example":"(BR) Ele ___ ligou ontem. <!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-clitic-limit","kind":"concept","format":"qa","priority":"essential","sourceSection":"Object Pronoun Placement","rule":"European Portuguese uses proclisis with triggers such as negation","example":"Does European Portuguese require a postverbal object pronoun even after não?","answer":"No. Negation attracts the pronoun before the verb, as in Não me ligou.","explanation":"European enclisis is common in affirmative main clauses, but triggers such as negation require proclisis."} -->
<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"rule-ep-enclisis","kind":"concept","format":"qa","priority":"optional","sourceSection":"Object Pronoun Placement","rule":"European affirmative main clauses commonly use enclisis","example":"In European Portuguese, where does an object pronoun usually go in an affirmative main clause with no trigger word?","answer":"After the verb, attached with a hyphen (ligou-me).","explanation":"PT Ele ligou-me ontem and Dá-me um copo de água; triggers such as negation put the pronoun before the verb (Não me ligou)."} -->

Brazilian Portuguese commonly places object pronouns **before** the verb (proclisis). European Portuguese commonly places them **after** in affirmative main clauses (enclisis), but triggers such as negation put them before: **Não me ligou**. These are not unconditional before/after rules:

- **BR: Me dá um copo de água.** (mee dah) — Give me a glass of water.
- **PT: Dá-me um copo de água.** — Give me a glass of water.

- **BR: Ele me ligou ontem.** (EH-lee mee lee-GOH) — He called me yesterday.
- **PT: Ele ligou-me ontem.** — He called me yesterday.

- **BR: Te amo.** (chee AH-moo) — I love you.
- **PT: Amo-te.** — I love you.

### "Ter" vs. "Haver" for Existence

<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-existence","kind":"rule","format":"qa","priority":"optional","sourceSection":"\"Ter\" vs. \"Haver\" for Existence","rule":"Informal Brazilian existential ter contrasts with standard haver","example":"What do Brazilian Tem muita gente aqui and European Há muita gente aqui both express?","answer":"There are a lot of people here; informal Brazilian speech often uses existential ter, while haver is standard in both varieties.","explanation":"These are usage preferences; haver is not forbidden in Brazil."} -->

Informal Brazilian speech often uses existential **ter** (to have) where European usage prefers **haver** (there is/are). **Haver** is standard in both varieties:

- **BR: Tem muita gente aqui.** (tehn MOO-ee-tah JEN-chee) — There are a lot of people here.
- **PT: Há muita gente aqui.** — There are a lot of people here.

- **BR: Não tem problema.** (nown tehn pro-BLEH-mah) — There's no problem.
- **PT: Não há problema.** — There's no problem.

### "É que" Construction

<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-e-que","kind":"rule","format":"qa","priority":"optional","sourceSection":"\"É que\" Construction","rule":"É que can frame or emphasize a question in both varieties","example":"What role does é que play in O que é que tu queres?","answer":"It emphasizes or frames the question; it is especially frequent in Portugal but also occurs in Brazil.","explanation":"É que is a question construction, not a different tense or a European-only expression."} -->

European Portuguese frequently uses **é que** for emphasis, especially in questions. This construction also occurs in Brazil:

- **PT: O que é que tu queres?** — What do you want?
- **BR: O que você quer?** — What do you want?

- **PT: Onde é que vais?** — Where are you going?
- **BR: Onde você vai?** — Where are you going?

## Spelling Differences

<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-spelling-consonant","kind":"concept","format":"qa","priority":"optional","sourceSection":"Spelling Differences","rule":"Pronunciation can preserve consonants in spelling variants","example":"Why can European facto retain c while Brazilian fato does not?","answer":"The c is pronounced in the European form; the agreement generally removes consonants that are silent in the relevant pronunciation.","explanation":"Post-agreement spelling can preserve variant forms when pronunciation differs."} -->
<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-spelling-ideia","kind":"rule","format":"cloze","priority":"optional","sourceSection":"Spelling Differences","rule":"Post-agreement ideia has no acute accent in either variety","example":"Write idea in post-agreement Portuguese: ___.","answer":"ideia","explanation":"Ideia has no acute accent under the current agreement in either variety."} -->
<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"variety-trema-history","kind":"concept","format":"qa","priority":"optional","sourceSection":"Spelling Differences","rule":"Portugal abolished the trema earlier than Brazil","example":"Did Portugal retain the trema until the same reform that removed it in Brazil?","answer":"No. Portugal had abolished it earlier; Brazil removed it from ordinary Portuguese words under the later agreement.","explanation":"The histories differ; the trema can still appear in foreign names and their derivatives."} -->

The 2009 Spelling Agreement (Acordo Ortográfico) unified most spelling, but some differences remain in practice:

| Feature | Brazilian | European |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| Consonants and pronunciation | fato (no c) | facto (c is pronounced in this form) |
| Accent on -eia | id**ei**a (no accent) | id**ei**a (no accent, post-agreement) |
| Dieresis (trema) | removed from ordinary Portuguese words under the agreement | abolished earlier |

Consonants are generally removed when silent in the relevant pronunciation; pronounced consonants may remain, permitting variety differences. The trema can still appear in foreign names and their derivatives.
