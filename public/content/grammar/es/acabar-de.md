# Acabar de + Infinitivo (Just Did Something)

**Acabar de + infinitivo** is the Spanish equivalent of English **"to have just (done something)"**. It expresses an action that finished a moment ago — very recent past. The construction is fixed and used constantly in everyday speech.

## The Core Construction

> **Acabar (conjugated) + de + infinitivo**

- **Acabo de comer** (ah-KAH-boh deh koh-MEHR) — I just ate / I've just eaten.
- **Acabamos de llegar** (ah-kah-BAH-mohs deh yeh-GAHR) — We just arrived.
- **Acaba de salir** — He/She just left.
- **Acaban de empezar** — They've just started.

Notice that English uses the perfect tense ("have just eaten"), but Spanish uses **present** + **acabar de**. Don't translate it word-for-word.

## Present Conjugation of Acabar (Regular -ar)

| Pronoun | Form | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| yo | **acabo** | ah-KAH-boh |
| tú | **acabas** | ah-KAH-bahs |
| él / ella / usted | **acaba** | ah-KAH-bah |

## Critical Rule: Only Present and Imperfect

This idiom **only works in two tenses**:

1. **Present** — "have just done" (just now)
2. **Imperfect** — "had just done" (just then, in the past)

Using the preterite **breaks the idiom**:

- ✅ **Acabo de comer** — I just ate.
- ✅ **Acababa de comer cuando llegaste** — I had just eaten when you arrived.
- ❌ **Acabé de comer** ≠ "I just ate." It means "I finished eating" — literal, not idiomatic.

This is the most important rule of the construction. Get it wrong and you change the meaning from "just did" to "finished doing".

## Imperfect: "Had Just Done X When…"

Use the **imperfect of acabar** + **de + infinitive** for the past version:

- **Acababa de llegar cuando sonó el teléfono** (ah-kah-BAH-bah deh yeh-GAHR KWAHN-doh soh-NOH ehl teh-LEH-foh-noh) — I had just arrived when the phone rang.
- **Acabábamos de cenar cuando empezó la lluvia** — We had just finished dinner when the rain started.
- **Acababan de irse** — They had just left.

This is the equivalent of English **past perfect + "just"**.

## Literal vs Idiomatic Acabar

**Acabar** by itself just means "to finish". The idiom only kicks in with **de + infinitive**:

| Form | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Acabo el libro** | I finish the book. |
| **Acabo de leer el libro** | I've just read the book. |
| **Acabé el libro** | I finished the book. *(literal)* |
| **Acabé de leer el libro** | I finished reading the book. *(literal completion)* |

So in the **preterite**, *acabar de + inf* simply means "finished doing X" — not "just did X". Native speakers don't use the preterite with this idiom for "just now"; they use the present.

## Mini-Dialogue

> — ¿Quieres comer algo? _(KYEH-rehs koh-MEHR AHL-goh)_
> — No, gracias, **acabo de** desayunar. _(noh, GRAH-syahs, ah-KAH-boh deh deh-sah-yoo-NAHR)_
> — Ah, vale. Oye, ¿y María? Quería hablar con ella.
> — **Acaba de** irse al mercado. Vuelve en una hora. _(ah-KAH-bah deh EER-seh ahl mehr-KAH-doh)_
> — Pues anoche, cuando llamé, **acababa de** salir también. ¡Qué mala suerte!
>
> Translation: "Want to eat something?" — "No thanks, I've just had breakfast." — "Oh, ok. Hey, where's María? I wanted to talk to her." — "She just left for the market. She'll be back in an hour." — "Well last night, when I called, she had just gone out too. What bad luck!"

Notice both the **present** (acabo de, acaba de) and the **imperfect** (acababa de) in the same conversation.

## Pronoun Placement

Object pronouns can go **before acabar** or **attached to the infinitive**:

- **Lo acabo de ver** = **Acabo de verlo** — I just saw him/it.
- **Te acabo de llamar** = **Acabo de llamarte** — I just called you.

Both are correct and equally common. The attached form is slightly more formal in writing.
