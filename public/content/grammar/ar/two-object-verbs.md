# Verbs with Two Objects

<!-- vocab-inventory: {"mode":"explicit"} -->

Some Arabic verbs naturally take **two direct objects**: a person who receives or learns something, and the thing received or learned. Both objects are in the accusative, even when English uses “to” or “for.”

## Core idea

<!-- grammar-card: {"id":"recipient-object","kind":"rule","format":"qa","priority":"essential","sourceSection":"Core idea","rule":"The recipient can be a direct object in an Arabic two-object construction.","example":"Does English “to the student” require an Arabic preposition in أَعْطَى الْمُعَلِّمُ الطَّالِبَ قَلَمًا?","answer":"No. The student is the first direct object; the pen is the second.","explanation":"Both complements are accusative in this permitted verb pattern; English prepositions do not determine Arabic case."} -->

In **أَعْطَى الْمُعَلِّمُ الطَّالِبَ قَلَمًا** (aʿṭā al-muʿallimu aṭ-ṭāliba qalaman) — “the teacher gave the student a pen,” **الطَّالِبَ** (aṭ-ṭāliba) and **قَلَمًا** (qalaman) are both objects. The first object is usually the recipient; the second is the item, information, or quality transferred.

## Usage notes

Some verbs also permit a prepositional recipient, especially with **إِلَى** (ilā) or **لِـ** (li-), depending on the verb and construction; these are not freely interchangeable after every verb in the table. The two-object pattern treats both complements as direct objects. With pronouns, the first object often attaches to the verb: **أَعْطَيْتُهُ كِتَابًا** (aʿṭaytuhu kitāban) — “I gave him a book.”

## Practice patterns

Turn the English “to someone” into a first object when the Arabic verb allows it:

- **سَأَلَتِ الْمُعَلِّمَةُ الطَّالِبَ سُؤَالًا** (saʾalati al-muʿallimatu aṭ-ṭāliba suʾālan) — the teacher asked the student a question.
- **أَلْبَسَتِ الْأُمُّ الطِّفْلَ قَمِيصًا** (albasati al-ummu aṭ-ṭifla qamīṣan) — the mother dressed the child in a shirt.
- **عَلَّمَ الْأَبُ بِنْتَهُ الْقِرَاءَةَ** (ʿallama al-abu bintahu al-qirāʾata) — the father taught his daughter reading.
