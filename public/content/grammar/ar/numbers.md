# Numbers & Counting (the agreement quirks)

Arabic numbers are famous for agreement quirks. You do not need to memorize every detail at once; learn the most common patterns, then let examples repeat.

## Core idea

One and two usually follow the noun and agree with it. From three to ten, the number often shows “opposite” gender from the counted noun, and the counted noun is plural genitive.

## Forms and examples

| Count | Example |
|---|---|
| one book | **كِتَابٌ وَاحِدٌ** (kitābun wāḥidun) — one book |
| one car | **سَيَّارَةٌ وَاحِدَةٌ** (sayyāratun wāḥidatun) — one car |
| two books | **كِتَابَانِ** (kitābāni) — two books, nominative |
| three books | **ثَلَاثَةُ كُتُبٍ** (thalāthatu kutubin) — three books |
| three cars | **ثَلَاثُ سَيَّارَاتٍ** (thalāthu sayyārātin) — three cars |
| ten students | **عَشَرَةُ طُلَّابٍ** (ʿasharatu ṭullābin) — ten students |

## Usage notes

The “opposite gender” rule for 3–10 is easier with examples. **كِتَابٌ** (kitābun) — book is masculine, so “three books” uses feminine-looking **ثَلَاثَةُ** (thalāthatu) — three. **سَيَّارَةٌ** (sayyāratun) — car is feminine, so “three cars” uses **ثَلَاثُ** (thalāthu) — three.

## Practice patterns

For 11–99, rules get more layered. For now, recognize useful forms:

- **أَحَدَ عَشَرَ كِتَابًا** (aḥada ʿashara kitāban) — eleven books.
- **عِشْرُونَ طَالِبًا** (ʿishrūna ṭāliban) — twenty students.
- **وَاحِدٌ وَعِشْرُونَ يَوْمًا** (wāḥidun wa-ʿishrūna yawman) — twenty-one days.
