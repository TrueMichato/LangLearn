# The Return Pronoun in Relative Clauses

<!-- vocab-inventory: {"mode":"explicit"} -->

Arabic relative clauses often contain a small pronoun that points back to the noun being described. Traditional grammar calls it the ʿāʾid, the return pronoun.

## Core idea

English can say “the book that I read” without repeating “it.” Arabic usually keeps the link: **الْكِتَابُ الَّذِي قَرَأْتُهُ** (al-kitābu alladhī qaraʾtuhu) — the book that I read it, meaning the book that I read.

| Subject | **جَاءَ الطَّالِبُ الَّذِي نَجَحَ** (jāʾa aṭ-ṭālibu alladhī najaḥa) — the student who succeeded came | an understood subject pronoun in نَجَحَ; no separate overt pronoun |

## Usage notes

If the head noun corresponds to the subject of the relative clause, the link may be the understood subject pronoun in its verb, so a separate overt pronoun is usually unnecessary. When the head noun is an object, possessor, or prepositional complement inside the clause, Arabic normally makes the link visible with an attached pronoun. Certain object returns can be omitted under grammatical conditions; the explicit patterns here are the safe learner models, not a claim that every omission is impossible.

## Practice patterns

<!-- vocab-card: {"id":"vocab-qissah","word":"الْقِصَّةُ","reading":"al-qiṣṣatu","meaning":"the story","priority":"optional","sourceSection":"Practice patterns"} -->

Notice how the suffix changes with the noun it points to:

- **هَذِهِ الْقِصَّةُ الَّتِي سَمِعْتُهَا** (hādhihi al-qiṣṣatu allatī samiʿtuhā) — this is the story that I heard.
- **هَذَا الْمُعَلِّمُ الَّذِي تَعَلَّمْتُ مِنْهُ** (hādhā al-muʿallimu alladhī taʿallamtu minhu) — this is the teacher from whom I learned.
- **تِلْكَ الْقَرْيَةُ الَّتِي عِشْتُ فِيهَا** (tilka al-qaryatu allatī ʿishtu fīhā) — that is the village in which I lived.
