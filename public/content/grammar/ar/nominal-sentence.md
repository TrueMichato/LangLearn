# The Nominal Sentence (there is no present "to be")

Arabic can make a complete present-tense sentence without a verb for “is / are.” This is one of the friendliest early wins: put a subject and a predicate together, and Arabic supplies the present “to be.”

## Core idea

A nominal sentence usually begins with a subject, the **مُبْتَدَأٌ** (mubtadaʾun) — topic / subject, followed by a predicate, the **خَبَرٌ** (khabarun) — information / predicate. In the present tense, there is no separate word for “is.”

## Forms and examples

| Arabic sentence | Translation |
|---|---|
| **الْبَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ** (al-baytu kabīrun) — the house is big | No word for “is” |
| **أَنَا طَالِبٌ** (anā ṭālibun) — I am a male student | Pronoun + predicate |
| **هِيَ مُدَرِّسَةٌ** (hiya mudarrisatun) — she is a teacher | Feminine predicate |
| **هٰذَا كِتَابٌ** (hādhā kitābun) — this is a book | Demonstrative subject |

## Usage notes

The subject is often definite, like **الْبَيْتُ** (al-baytu) — the house, while the predicate is often indefinite, like **كَبِيرٌ** (kabīrun) — big. That contrast helps Arabic say “the house is big” rather than “the big house.”

## Practice patterns

Compare phrase vs. sentence:

- **الْبَيْتُ الْكَبِيرُ** (al-baytu al-kabīru) — the big house.
- **الْبَيْتُ كَبِيرٌ** (al-baytu kabīrun) — the house is big.

The second example is a full sentence because the predicate is indefinite.
