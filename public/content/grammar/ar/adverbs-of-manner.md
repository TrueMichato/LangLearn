# Adverbs of Manner & the Mafʿūl Muṭlaq (cognate accusative)

Arabic often expresses manner with an accusative word or phrase rather than with an English-style adverb ending. Two useful tools are the ḥāl and the mafʿūl muṭlaq.

## Core idea

حَالٌ (ḥālun) — circumstantial accusative describes the state or manner of the subject or object. مَفْعُولٌ مُطْلَقٌ (mafʿūlun muṭlaqun) — cognate accusative uses a verbal noun from the same root as the verb.

| Tool | Arabic example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ḥāl | جَاءَ مُسْرِعًا (jāʾa musriʿan) — he came quickly | state/manner |
| ḥāl | رَجَعَتْ سَعِيدَةً (rajaʿat saʿīdatan) — she returned happy | state of subject |
| mafʿūl muṭlaq | ضَحِكَ ضَحِكًا شَدِيدًا (ḍaḥika ḍaḥikan shadīdan) — he laughed intensely | emphasis/type |
| mafʿūl muṭlaq | مَشَى مَشْيًا بَطِيئًا (mashā mashyan baṭīʾan) — he walked slowly | manner/type of action |

## Adverbs of manner through ḥāl

The ḥāl is often indefinite and accusative: وَقَفَ الطَّالِبُ هَادِئًا (waqafa aṭ-ṭālibu hādiʾan) — the student stood calmly. The word هَادِئًا (hādiʾan) — calmly / calm describes the subject’s state during the action.

## The cognate accusative

The mafʿūl muṭlaq repeats the verb’s root as a verbal noun. It can emphasize the action, count it, or describe its kind: فَرِحْتُ فَرَحًا كَبِيرًا (fariḥtu faraḥan kabīran) — I rejoiced greatly; سَجَدَ سَجْدَةً وَاحِدَةً (sajada sajdatan wāḥidatan) — he prostrated one prostration.

- كَتَبَ كِتَابَةً وَاضِحَةً (kataba kitābatan wāḍiḥatan) — he wrote clearly.
- سَارَ سَيْرًا سَرِيعًا (sāra sayran sarīʿan) — he traveled quickly.
- تَكَلَّمَ كَلَامًا جَمِيلًا (takallama kalāman jamīlan) — he spoke beautifully.

> English may use an adverb, but Arabic may prefer an accusative noun phrase that describes the action itself.
