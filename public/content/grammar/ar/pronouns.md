# Personal Pronouns

Arabic personal pronouns mark person, gender, and number more richly than English. Start with the most common singular and plural forms; the dual will get its own lesson later.

## Core idea

Independent pronouns stand alone as subjects. In present nominal sentences, they often mean “I am,” “you are,” and so on without adding a verb.

## Forms and examples

| Pronoun | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **أَنَا** (anā) — I | first person singular |
| **أَنْتَ** (anta) — you, masculine singular | speaking to one male |
| **أَنْتِ** (anti) — you, feminine singular | speaking to one female |
| **هُوَ** (huwa) — he / it masculine | third masculine singular |
| **هِيَ** (hiya) — she / it feminine | third feminine singular |
| **نَحْنُ** (naḥnu) — we | first person plural |
| **أَنْتُمْ** (antum) — you, masculine plural / mixed | plural address |
| **هُمْ** (hum) — they, masculine / mixed | plural reference |
| **هُنَّ** (hunna) — they, feminine | feminine plural reference |

## Usage notes

Because verbs already carry subject endings, Arabic often drops pronouns with verbs. But pronouns are very useful for emphasis and for verbless sentences like **أَنَا سَعِيدٌ** (anā saʿīdun) — I am happy, masculine.

## Practice patterns

Gender in “you” matters:

- **أَنْتَ طَالِبٌ** (anta ṭālibun) — you are a male student.
- **أَنْتِ طَالِبَةٌ** (anti ṭālibatun) — you are a female student.
- **نَحْنُ طُلَّابٌ** (naḥnu ṭullābun) — we are students.
