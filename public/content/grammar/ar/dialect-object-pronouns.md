# Object & Possessive Suffixes Across Dialects

Arabic dialects keep the old idea of suffix pronouns: a short ending can mean my, your, his, her, me, you, him, or her. The forms are familiar from MSA, but pronunciation and some details change by region.

## The shared pattern

A suffix on a noun is possessive. A suffix on a verb is usually an object. MSA writes many final vowels that dialect speech often shortens or drops.

| Function | MSA | Common dialect idea |
|---|---|---|
| my book | كِتَابِي (kitābī) | كِتَابِي (kitābī) or كْتَابِي (ktābī) |
| your book, masculine | كِتَابُكَ (kitābuka) | كِتَابَك (kitābak) |
| his book | كِتَابُهُ (kitābuhu) | كِتَابُهْ (kitābu, kitābo, or kitābah) |
| I saw him | رَأَيْتُهُ (raʾaytuhu) | شُفْتُهْ (shuftu or shufto) |

## What changes from MSA

Three changes are especially common. First, final short vowels often disappear: كِتَابُكَ (kitābuka) becomes كِتَابَك (kitābak) or بَيْتَك (bētak). Second, the masculine third-person suffix may sound like -u, -o, or -ah depending on region and surrounding sounds. Third, many dialects use a different verb stem from MSA: رَأَى (raʾā) — saw becomes شَافَ (shāfa) in many dialects.

- MSA: رَأَيْتُهَا فِي السُّوقِ (raʾaytuhā fī as-sūqi) — I saw her in the market.
- Egyptian-like: شُفْتُهَا فِي السُّوق (shuftahā fi-s-sūʾ) — I saw her in the market.
- Levantine-like: شِفْتُهَا بِالسُّوق (shiftahā bi-s-sūʾ) — I saw her in the market.
