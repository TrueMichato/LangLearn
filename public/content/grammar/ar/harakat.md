# Short Vowels, Sukūn & Shadda (Tashkīl)

Tashkīl is the set of small marks that tells you short vowels and doubling. Many real-world Arabic texts omit them, but learning with them is a kindness to your future reading self.

## Core idea

The three short vowels are **فَتْحَةٌ** (fatḥatun) — a, **كَسْرَةٌ** (kasratun) — i, and **ضَمَّةٌ** (ḍammatun) — u. **سُكُونٌ** (sukūnun) — no vowel silences a consonant, and **شَدَّةٌ** (shaddatun) — doubling makes a consonant long / doubled.

## Forms and examples

| Mark | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| fatḥa | **كَتَبَ** (kataba) — he wrote | short a |
| kasra | **كِتَابٌ** (kitābun) — book | short i, plus long ā |
| ḍamma | **كُتُبٌ** (kutubun) — books | short u |
| sukūn | **دَرْسٌ** (darsun) — lesson | **رْ** (rْ) — r has no vowel |
| shadda | **مُدَرِّسٌ** (mudarrisun) — teacher | **رِّ** (rri) — doubled r |

## Usage notes

A shadda includes a doubled consonant. In **مُدَرِّسٌ** (mudarrisun) — teacher, the sound is not *mudaris* but *mudarris*. Sukūn is the opposite: pause on the consonant, as in **بَيْتٌ** (baytun) — house.

## Practice patterns

Tashkīl can change meaning:

- **كَتَبَ** (kataba) — he wrote.
- **كُتِبَ** (kutiba) — it was written.
- **عَلِمَ** (ʿalima) — he knew.
- **عَلَّمَ** (ʿallama) — he taught.
