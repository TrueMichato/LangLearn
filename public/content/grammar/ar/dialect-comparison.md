# One Phrase, Six Dialects

Arabic varieties share a large core, but everyday phrases can change quickly across regions. This lesson compares **الْفُصْحَى** (al-fuṣḥā) — Modern Standard Arabic, **الْمِصْرِيَّةُ** (al-miṣriyyatu) — Egyptian Arabic, **الشَّامِيَّةُ** (ash-shāmiyyatu) — Levantine Arabic, **الْخَلِيجِيَّةُ** (al-khalījiyyatu) — Gulf Arabic, **الْعِرَاقِيَّةُ** (al-ʿirāqiyyatu) — Iraqi Arabic, and **الْمَغَارِبِيَّةُ** (al-maghāribiyyatu) — Maghrebi Arabic.

## Core idea

MSA is the shared formal standard. Dialects are normal spoken systems with their own grammar, sound changes, and vocabulary. Vocalization below is approximate for learning; local pronunciation varies.

## Everyday phrase table

| Meaning | MSA | Egyptian | Levantine | Gulf | Iraqi | Maghrebi |

## Usage notes

Some differences are lexical: MSA **أُرِيدُ** (ʾurīdu) — I want, Egyptian **عَايِزْ** (ʿāyiz) — wanting, Levantine **بِدِّي** (biddī) — I want. Others are sound changes: **قَهْوَةٌ** (qahwatun) — coffee may sound closer to **gāhwa** in some Gulf speech.

## Reading comparisons kindly

Do not rank dialects as broken MSA. Each dialect has rules. Compare phrase by phrase, and notice recurring markers:

- **بِـ** (bi-) — present marker appears in many Levantine forms.
- **مِشْ** (mish) — not appears in Egyptian and some Levantine contexts.
- **مَا ... ـش** (mā ... -sh) — circumfix negation appears in Maghrebi and Egyptian patterns.
- **شْلُونَك؟** (shlōnak?) — how are you? is common in Gulf and Iraqi speech.
